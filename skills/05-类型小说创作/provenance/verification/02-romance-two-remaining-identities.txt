32	Mr and Mrs Openshaw came from Manchester to settle in London. He
33	had been, what is called in Lancashire, a salesman for a large
34	manufacturing firm, who were extending their business, and opening a
35	warehouse in the city; where Mr Openshaw was now to superintend their
36	affairs. He rather enjoyed the change; having a kind of curiosity
37	about London, which he had never yet been able to gratify in his brief
38	visits to the metropolis. At the same time, he had an odd, shrewd
39	contempt for the inhabitants, whom he always pictured to himself as
40	fine, lazy people, caring nothing but for fashion and aristocracy, and
41	lounging away their days in Bond Street, and such places; ruining good
42	English, and ready in their turn to despise him as a provincial. The
43	hours that the men of business kept in the city scandalized him too,
44	accustomed as he was to the early dinners of Manchester folk and
45	the consequently far longer evenings. Still, he was pleased to go to
46	London, though he would not for the world have confessed it, even to
47	himself, and always spoke of the step to his friends as one demanded
48	of him by the interests of his employers, and sweetened to him by a
49	considerable increase of salary. This, indeed, was so liberal that he
50	might have been justified in taking a much larger house than the
51	one he did, had he not thought himself bound to set an example to
52	Londoners of how little a Manchester man of business cared for show.
53	Inside, however, he furnished it with an unusual degree of comfort,
54	and, in the winter-time, he insisted on keeping up as large fires as
55	the grates would allow, in every room where the temperature was in
56	the least chilly. Moreover, his northern sense of hospitality was such
57	that, if he were at home, he could hardly suffer a visitor to leave
58	the house without forcing meat and drink upon him. Every servant in
59	the house was well warmed, well fed, and kindly treated; for their
60	master scorned all petty saving in aught that conduced to comfort;
61	while he amused himself by following out all his accustomed habits and
62	individual ways, in defiance of what any of his new neighbours might
63	think.
64	
65	His wife was a pretty, gentle woman, of suitable age and character. He
66	was forty-two, she thirty-five. He was loud and decided; she soft and
67	yielding. They had two children; or rather, I should say, she had two;
68	for the elder, a girl of eleven, was Mrs Openshaw's child by Frank
69	Wilson, her first husband. The younger was a little boy, Edwin, who
70	could just prattle, and to whom his father delighted to speak in the
71	broadest and most unintelligible Lancashire dialect, in order to keep
72	up what he called the true Saxon accent.
73

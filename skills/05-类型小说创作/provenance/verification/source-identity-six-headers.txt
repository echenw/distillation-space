26: More generally known, perhaps, than any other Hindoo legend, is the
27: story of the demon, RÁHU, who brings about ECLIPSES, by devouring the
28: Sun and Moon. For when the gods had upchurned the nectar, the delectable
29: Butter of the Brine, Ráhu's mouth watered at the very sight of it: and
30: "in the guise of a god" he mingled unperceived among them, to partake.
31: But the Sun and Moon, the watchful Eyes of Night and Day, detected him,
32: and told Wishnu, who cast at him his discus, and cut his body from his
33: head: but not until the nectar was on the way down his throat. Hence,
34: though the body died, the head became immortal: and ever since, a thing
35: unique, "no body and all head," a byword among philosophers, he takes
36: revenge on Sun and Moon, the great Taletellers, by "gripping" them in
37: his horrid jaws, and holding on, till he is tired, or can be persuaded
38: to let go. Hence, in some parts of India, the doleful shout of the
39: country people at eclipses: _Chor do! chor do[1]!_ and hence, also, the
40: primary and surface meaning of our title: _A Digit of the Moon in the
41: Demon's grip_: in plain English, _an eclipse of the moon_. And yet,
42: legend though it be, there is something in the old mythological way of
43: putting the case, which describes the situation in eclipses, far better
44: than our arid scientific prose. I shall not easily forget, how, as we
45: slid like ghosts at midnight, through the middle of the desert, along
46: the Suez Canal[2], I watched the ghastly pallor of the wan unhappy moon,
47: as the horrible shadow crept slowly over her face, stealing away her
48: beauty, and turning the lone and level sands that stretched away below
49: to a weird and ashy blue, as though covering the earth with a sepulchral
50: sympathetic pall. For we caught the "griesly terror," Ráhu, at his
51: horrid work, towards the end of May, four years ago.

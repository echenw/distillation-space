58	CHAPTER I
59	
60	
61	Margaret Dashwood was an observer of life. Her temper was calm, her
62	manner gentle, and she was able to listen to the accounts other
63	people gave of their activities without the appearance of fatigue.
64	The circumstances of her life up to the age of seventeen had combined
65	to increase in her these qualities, so valuable to her acquaintance,
66	so agreeable to herself, and so baffling to those desiring a nearer
67	intimacy. She was the youngest of three daughters, not so accomplished
68	and self-reliant as Elinor, not so handsome and impulsive as Marianne,
69	and less attractive than either, if to be immediately noticed is to be
70	attractive.
71	
72	Their mother was a widow, whose income, though possibly equal to her
73	expenditure, was consistently below her wishes, and the three Miss
74	Dashwoods were obliged to suit their requirements to their mother’s
75	purse rather than to her heart.
76	
77	Mr. and Mrs. Dashwood had lived for ten years with his uncle, the
78	owner of a large estate in Sussex to which Mr. Dashwood was the heir.
79	The property was inherited, but enjoyed for only a twelvemonth, and
80	Mrs. Dashwood became a widow with a small income.
81	
82	The idea of wealth takes root quickly in the mind, and Mrs. Dashwood
83	was not easily convinced that she was now unable to afford indulgences
84	for her daughters. Her own tastes were simple, or became so after her
85	change of situation; and, in order that Elinor and Marianne might be
86	suitably attired and escorted, her own pin-money was severely taxed.
87	Margaret, as the youngest daughter and not yet grown up, had a more
88	personal experience of the family economies than her sisters, and she
89	learned more of the meaning of the word “poor” than either of them was
90	ever to know.
91

25	Hathercourt Church is not beautiful, though the internal evidence in
26	favour of its having at one time been so is considerable.  It has
27	suffered sorely at the hands of plasterers and white-washers; yet the
28	utmost efforts of these misguided people have not altogether succeeded
29	in effacing the traces of a better state of things--there is still
30	grandeur in the sweep of the lofty roof, oak-raftered behind its dingy
31	white covering; still "meaning and mystery" in the quaintly varying
32	windows; much satisfaction for the learned in such matters, and indeed
33	for the unlearned too, in the unmistakable beauty of the carved screen,
34	the one object untampered with since the days when it gladdened the eyes
35	of the ancient men who fashioned it, long, long ago.
36	
37	A _very_ long "long ago" that time used to seem to Mary Western when, in
38	the intervals of her attention to the service, she sometimes dreamed of
39	those far-away days.  She was not much given to dreaming, but in
40	Hathercourt Church there were circumstances under which the temptation
41	became irresistible.
42	
43	After a course of years the words of the morning service, especially
44	when read, Sunday after Sunday, by the same familiar voice with
45	precisely the same intonations, are apt to grow monotonous; and had Mary
46	not occasionally allowed her thoughts to go wool-gathering, the chances
47	are that her brown straw hat would have been seen to nod, and she might
48	have fallen asleep altogether.  For that part of Sunday morning which
49	preceded their appearance in church was a tiring and trying ordeal to
50	the elder daughters of the Western household.  There was the early class
51	at the school, there were "the boys" at home to keep peace among, there
52	were the very little children in the nursery to coax into unwonted
53	quiet, for on Sunday mornings "papa" really must not be disturbed, and
54	mamma, "poor mamma," looked to her girls to do their part in helping
55	her.

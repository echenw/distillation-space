31: I. THE FACE IN THE TARGET
32: 
33: Harold March, the rising reviewer and social critic, was walking
34: vigorously across a great tableland of moors and commons, the
35: horizon of which was fringed with the far-off woods of the famous
36: estate of Torwood Park. He was a good-looking young man in tweeds,
37: with very pale curly hair and pale clear eyes. Walking in wind and
38: sun in the very landscape of liberty, he was still young enough to
39: remember his politics and not merely try to forget them. For his
40: errand at Torwood Park was a political one; it was the place of
41: appointment named by no less a person than the Chancellor of the
42: Exchequer, Sir Howard Horne, then introducing his so-called
43: Socialist budget, and prepared to expound it in an interview with so
44: promising a penman. Harold March was the sort of man who knows
45: everything about politics, and nothing about politicians. He also
46: knew a great deal about art, letters, philosophy, and general
47: culture; about almost everything, indeed, except the world he was
48: living in.
49: 
50: Abruptly, in the middle of those sunny and windy flats, he came upon
51: a sort of cleft almost narrow enough to be called a crack in the
52: land. It was just large enough to be the water-course for a small
53: stream which vanished at intervals under green tunnels of
54: undergrowth, as if in a dwarfish forest. Indeed, he had an odd
55: feeling as if he were a giant looking over the valley of the
56: pygmies. When he dropped into the hollow, however, the impression
57: was lost; the rocky banks, though hardly above the height of a
58: cottage, hung over and had the profile of a precipice. As he began
59: to wander down the course of the stream, in idle but romantic
60: curiosity, and saw the water shining in short strips between the
61: great gray boulders and bushes as soft as great green mosses, he
62: fell into quite an opposite vein of fantasy. It was rather as if the
63: earth had opened and swallowed him into a sort of underworld of
64: dreams. And when he became conscious of a human figure dark against
65: the silver stream, sitting on a large boulder and looking rather
66: like a large bird, it was perhaps with some of the premonitions
67: proper to a man who meets the strangest friendship of his life.
68: 
69: The man was apparently fishing; or at least was fixed in a
70: fisherman's attitude with more than a fisherman's immobility. March
71: was able to examine the man almost as if he had been a statue for
72: some minutes before the statue spoke. He was a tall, fair man,
73: cadaverous, and a little lackadaisical, with heavy eyelids and a
74: highbridged nose. When his face was shaded with his wide white hat,
75: his light mustache and lithe figure gave him a look of youth. But
76: the Panama lay on the moss beside him; and the spectator could see
77: that his brow was prematurely bald; and this, combined with a
78: certain hollowness about the eyes, had an air of headwork and even
79: headache. But the most curious thing about him, realized after a
80: short scrutiny, was that, though he looked like a fisherman, he was
81: not fishing.
82: 
83: He was holding, instead of a rod, something that might have been a
84: landing-net which some fishermen use, but which was much more like
85: the ordinary toy net which children carry, and which they generally
86: use indifferently for shrimps or butterflies. He was dipping this
87: into the water at intervals, gravely regarding its harvest of weed
88: or mud, and emptying it out again.
89: 
90: "No, I haven't caught anything," he remarked, calmly, as if

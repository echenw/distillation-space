31: Toward sundown, in the murky drizzle, the man who called himself Ord
32: brought Lieutenant colonel William Barrett Travis word that the Mexican
33: light cavalry had completely invested Bexar, and that some light guns were
34: being set up across the San Antonio River. Even as he spoke, there was a
35: flash and bang from the west, and a shell screamed over the old mission
36: walls. Travis looked worried.
37: 
38: [Illustration:]
39: 
40: "What kind of guns?" he asked.
41: 
42: "Nothing to worry about, sir," Ord said. "Only a few one-pounders, nothing
43: of respectable siege caliber. General Santa Anna has had to move too fast
44: for any big stuff to keep up." Ord spoke in his odd accent. After all, he
45: was a Britainer, or some other kind of foreigner. But he spoke good
46: Spanish, and he seemed to know everything. In the four or five days since
47: he had appeared he had become very useful to Travis.
48: 
49: Frowning, Travis asked, "How many Mexicans, do you think, Ord?"
50: 
51: "Not more than a thousand, now," the dark-haired, blue-eyed young man said
52: confidently. "But when the main body arrives, there'll be four, five
53: thousand."
54: 
55: Travis shook his head. "How do you get all this information, Ord? You
56: recite it like you had read it all some place--like it were history."
57: 
58: Ord merely smiled. "Oh, I don't know _everything_, colonel. That is why I
59: had to come here. There is so much we don't know about what happened.... I
60: mean, sir, what will happen--in the Alamo." His sharp eyes grew puzzled for
61: an instant. "And some things don't seem to match up, somehow--"
62: 
63: Travis looked at him sympathetically. Ord talked queerly at times, and
64: Travis suspected he was a bit deranged. This was understandable, for the
65: man was undoubtedly a Britainer aristocrat, a refugee from Napoleon's
66: thousand-year Empire. Travis had heard about the detention camps and the
67: charcoal ovens ... but once, when he had mentioned the _Empereur's_ sack of
68: London in '06, Ord had gotten a very queer look in his eyes, as if he had
69: forgotten completely.
70: 
71: But John Ord, or whatever his name was, seemed to be the only man in the
72: Texas forces who understood what William Barrett Travis was trying to do.
73: Now Travis looked around at the thick adobe wall surrounding the old
74: mission in which they stood. In the cold, yellowish twilight even the
75: flaring cook fires of his hundred and eighty-two men could not dispel the
76: ghostly air that clung to the old place. Travis shivered involuntarily. But
77: the walls were thick, and they could turn one-pounders. He asked, "What was
78: it you called this place, Ord ... the Mexican name?"
79: 
80: "The Alamo, sir." A slow, steady excitement seemed to burn in the
81: Britainer's bright eyes. "Santa Anna won't forget that name, you can be
82: sure. You'll want to talk to the other officers now, sir? About the message
83: we drew up for Sam Houston?"
84: 
85: "Yes, of course," Travis said absently. He watched Ord head for the walls.
86: No doubt about it, Ord understood what William Barrett Travis was trying to
87: do here. So few of the others seemed to care.
88: 
89: Travis was suddenly very glad that John Ord had shown up when he did.
90: 
91: On the walls, Ord found the man he sought, broad-shouldered and tall in a
92: fancy Mexican jacket. "The commandant's compliments, sir, and he desires
93: your presence in the chapel."
94: 
95: The big man put away the knife with which he had been whittling. The
96: switchblade snicked back and disappeared into a side pocket of the jacket,
97: while Ord watched it with fascinated eyes. "What's old Bill got his
98: britches hot about this time?" the big man asked.
99: 
100: "I wouldn't know, sir," Ord said stiffly and moved on.
101: 
102: _Bang-bang-bang_ roared the small Mexican cannon from across the river.
103: _Pow-pow-pow!_ The little balls only chipped dust from the thick adobe
104: walls. Ord smiled.
105: 
106: He found the second man he sought, a lean man with a weathered face,
107: leaning against a wall and chewing tobacco. This man wore a long, fringed,
108: leather lounge jacket, and he carried a guitar slung beside his Rock Island
109: rifle. He squinted up at Ord. "I know ... I know," he muttered. "Willy
110: Travis is in an uproar again. You reckon that colonel's commission that
111: Congress up in Washington-on-the-Brazos give him swelled his head?"
112: 
113: Rather stiffly, Ord said, "Colonel, the commandant desires an officers'
114: conference in the chapel, now." Ord was somewhat annoyed. He had not
115: realized he would find these Americans so--distasteful. Hardly preferable
116: to Mexicans, really. Not at all as he had imagined.
117: 
118: For an instant he wished he had chosen Drake and the Armada instead of this
119: pack of ruffians--but no, he had never been able to stand sea sickness. He
120: couldn't have taken the Channel, not even for five minutes.
121: 
122: And there was no changing now. He had chosen this place and time carefully,
123: at great expense--actually, at great risk, for the X-4-A had aborted twice,
124: and he had had a hard time bringing her in. But it had got him here at
125: last. And, because for a historian he had always been an impetuous and
126: daring man, he grinned now, thinking of the glory that was to come. And he
127: was a participant--much better than a ringside seat! Only he would have to
128: be careful, at the last, to slip away.
129: 
130: John Ord knew very well how this coming battle had ended, back here in
131: 1836.
132: 
133: He marched back to William Barrett Travis, clicked heels smartly. Travis'
134: eyes glowed; he was the only senior officer here who loved military
135: punctilio. "Sir, they are on the way."
136: 
137: "Thank you, Ord," Travis hesitated a moment. "Look, Ord. There will be a
138: battle, as we know. I know so little about you. If something should happen
139: to you, is there anyone to write? Across the water?"
140: 
141: Ord grinned. "No, sir. I'm afraid my ancestor wouldn't understand."
142: 
143: Travis shrugged. Who was he to say that Ord was crazy? In this day and age,
144: any man with vision was looked on as mad. Sometimes he felt closer to Ord
145: than to the others.
146: 
147:        *       *       *       *       *
148: 
149: The two officers Ord had summoned entered the chapel. The big man in the
150: Mexican jacket tried to dominate the wood table at which they sat. He
151: towered over the slender, nervous Travis, but the commandant,
152: straight-backed and arrogant, did not give an inch. "Boys, you know Santa
153: Anna has invested us. We've been fired on all day--" He seemed to be
154: listening for something. _Wham!_ Outside, a cannon split the dusk with
155: flame and sound as it fired from the walls. "There is my answer!"
156: 
157: The man in the lounge coat shrugged. "What I want to know is what our
158: orders are. What does old Sam say? Sam and me were in Congress once. Sam's
159: got good sense; he can smell the way the wind's blowin'." He stopped
160: speaking and hit his guitar a few licks. He winked across the table at the
161: officer in the Mexican jacket who took out his knife. "Eh, Jim?"
162: 
163: "Right," Jim said. "Sam's a good man, although I don't think he ever met a
164: payroll."
165: 
166: "General Houston's leaving it up to me," Travis told them.
167: 
168: "Well, that's that," Jim said unhappily. "So what you figurin' to do,
169: Bill?"
170: 
171: Travis stood up in the weak, flickering candlelight, one hand on the
172: polished hilt of his saber. The other two men winced, watching him.
173: "Gentlemen, Houston's trying to pull his militia together while he falls
174: back. You know, Texas was woefully unprepared for a contest at arms. The
175: general's idea is to draw Santa Anna as far into Texas as he can, then hit
176: him when he's extended, at the right place, and right time. But Houston
177: needs more time--Santa Anna's moved faster than any of us anticipated.
178: Unless we can stop the Mexican Army and take a little steam out of them,
179: General Houston's in trouble."
180: 
181: Jim flicked the knife blade in and out. "Go on."
182: 
183: "This is where we come in, gentlemen. Santa Anna can't leave a force of one
184: hundred eighty men in his rear. If we hold fast, he must attack us. But he
185: has no siege equipment, not even large field cannon." Travis' eye gleamed.
186: "Think of it, boys! He'll have to mount a frontal attack, against protected
187: American riflemen. Ord, couldn't your Englishers tell him a few things
188: about that!"
189: 
190: "Whoa, now," Jim barked. "Billy, anybody tell you there's maybe four or
191: five thousand Mexicaners comin'?"
192: 
193: "Let them come. Less will leave!"
194: 
195: But Jim, sour-faced turned to the other man. "Davey? You got something to
196: say?"
197: 
198: "Hell, yes. How do we get out, after we done pinned Santa Anna down? You
199: thought of that, Billy boy?"
200: 
201: Travis shrugged. "There is an element of grave risk, of course. Ord,
202: where's the document, the message you wrote up for me? Ah, thank you."
203: Travis cleared his throat. "Here's what I'm sending on to general Houston."
204: He read, "Commandancy of the Alamo, February 24, 1836 ... are you sure of
205: that date, Ord?"
206: 
207: "Oh, I'm sure of that," Ord said.
208: 
209: "Never mind--if you're wrong we can change it later. 'To the People of
210: Texas and all Americans in the World. Fellow Freemen and Compatriots! I am
211: besieged with a thousand or more Mexicans under Santa Anna. I have
212: sustained a continual bombardment for many hours but have not lost a man.
213: The enemy has demanded surrender at discretion, otherwise, the garrison is
214: to be put to the sword, if taken. I have answered the demand with a cannon
215: shot, and our flag still waves proudly over the walls. I shall never
216: surrender or retreat. Then, I call on you in the name of liberty, of
217: patriotism and everything dear to the American character--" He paused,
218: frowning, "This language seems pretty old-fashioned, Ord--"
219: 
220: "Oh, no, sir. That's exactly right," Ord murmured.
221: 
222: "'... To come to our aid with all dispatch. The enemy is receiving
223: reinforcements daily and will no doubt increase to three or four thousand
224: in four or five days. If this call is neglected, I am determined to sustain
225: myself as long as possible and die like a soldier who never forgets what is
226: due his honor or that of his homeland. VICTORY OR DEATH!'"
227: 
228:        *       *       *       *       *
229: 
230: Travis stopped reading, looked up. "Wonderful! Wonderful!" Ord breathed.
231: "The greatest words of defiance ever written in the English tongue--and so
232: much more literate than that chap at Bascogne."
233: 
234: "You mean to send that?" Jim gasped.
235: 
236: The man called Davey was holding his head in his hands.
237: 
238: "You object, Colonel Bowie?" Travis asked icily.
239: 
240: "Oh, cut that 'colonel' stuff, Bill," Bowie said. "It's only a National
241: Guard title, and I like 'Jim' better, even though I am a pretty important
242: man. Damn right I have an objection! Why, that message is almost
243: aggressive. You'd think we wanted to fight Santa Anna! You want us to be
244: marked down as warmongers? It'll give us trouble when we get to the
245: negotiation table--"
246: 
247: Travis' head turned. "Colonel Crockett?"
248: 
249: "What Jim says goes for me, too. And this: I'd change that part about all
250: Americans, et cetera. You don't want anybody to think we think we're better
251: than the Mexicans. After all, Americans are a minority in the world. Why
252: not make it 'all men who love security?' That'd have world-wide appeal--"
253: 
254: "Oh, Crockett," Travis hissed.
255: 
256: Crockett stood up. "Don't use that tone of voice to me, Billy Travis! That
257: piece of paper you got don't make you no better'n us. I ran for Congress
258: twice, and won. I know what the people want--"
259: 
260: "What the people want doesn't mean a damn right now," Travis said harshly.
261: "Don't you realize the tyrant is at the gates?"
262: 
263: Crockett rolled his eyes heavenward. "Never thought I'd hear a good
264: American say that! Billy, you'll never run for office--"
265: 
266: Bowie held up a hand, cutting into Crockett's talk. "All right, Davey. Hold
267: up. You ain't runnin' for Congress now. Bill, the main thing I don't like
268: in your whole message is that part about victory or death. That's got to
269: go. Don't ask us to sell that to the troops!"
270: 
271: Travis closed his eyes briefly. "Boys, listen. We don't have to tell the
272: men about this. They don't need to know the real story until it's too late
273: for them to get out. And then we shall cover ourselves with such glory that
274: none of us shall ever be forgotten. Americans are the best fighters in the
275: world when they are trapped. They teach this in the Foot School back on the
276: Chatahoochee. And if we die, to die for one's country is sweet--"
277: 
278: "Hell with that," Crockett drawled. "I don't mind dyin', but not for these
279: big landowners like Jim Bowie here. I just been thinkin'--I don't own
280: nothing in Texas."
281: 
282: "I resent that," Bowie shouted. "You know very well I volunteered, after I
283: sent my wife off to Acapulco to be with her family." With an effort, he
284: calmed himself. "Look, Travis. I have some reputation as a fighting
285: man--you know I lived through the gang wars back home. It's obvious this
286: Alamo place is indefensible, even if we had a thousand men."
287: 
288: "But we must delay Santa Anna at all costs--"
289: 
290: Bowie took out a fine, dark Mexican cigar and whittled at it with his
291: blade. Then he lit it, saying around it, "All right, let's all calm down.
292: Nothing a group of good men can't settle around a table. Now listen. I got
293: in with this revolution at first because I thought old Emperor Iturbide
294: would listen to reason and lower taxes. But nothin's worked out, because
295: hot-heads like you, Travis, queered the deal. All this yammerin' about
296: liberty! Mexico is a Republic, under an Emperor, not some kind of
297: democracy, and we can't change that. Let's talk some sense before it's too
298: late. We're all too old and too smart to be wavin' the flag like it's the
299: Fourth of July. Sooner or later, we're goin' to have to sit down and talk
300: with the Mexicans. And like Davey said, I own a million hectares, and I've
301: always paid minimum wage, and my wife's folks are way up there in the
302: Imperial Government of the Republic of Mexico. That means I got influence
303: in all the votin' groups, includin' the American Immigrant, since I'm a
304: minority group member myself. I think I can talk to Santa Anna, and even to
305: old Iturbide. If we sign a treaty now with Santa Anna, acknowledge the law
306: of the land, I think our lives and property rights will be respected--" He
307: cocked an eye toward Crockett.
308: 
309: "Makes sense, Jim. That's the way we do it in Congress. Compromise,
310: everybody happy. We never allowed ourselves to be led nowhere we didn't
311: want to go, I can tell you! And Bill, you got to admit that we're in better
312: bargaining position if we're out in the open, than if old Santa Anna's got
313: us penned up in this old Alamo."
314: 
315: "Ord," Travis said despairingly. "Ord, you understand. Help me! Make them
316: listen!"
317: 
318:        *       *       *       *       *
319: 
320: Ord moved into the candlelight, his lean face sweating. "Gentlemen, this is
321: all wrong! It doesn't happen this way--"
322: 
323: Crockett sneered, "Who asked you, Ord? I'll bet you ain't even got a poll
324: tax!"
325: 
326: Decisively, Bowie said, "We're free men, Travis, and we won't be led around
327: like cattle. How about it, Davey? Think you could handle the rear guard, if
328: we try to move out of here?"
329: 
330: "Hell, yes! Just so we're movin'!"
331: 
332: "O.K. Put it to a vote of the men outside. Do we stay, and maybe get
333: croaked, or do we fall back and conserve our strength until we need it?
334: Take care of it, eh, Davey?"
335: 
336: Crockett picked up his guitar and went outside.
337: 
338: Travis roared, "This is insubordination! Treason!" He drew his saber, but
339: Bowie took it from him and broke it in two. Then the big man pulled his
340: knife.
341: 
342: "Stay back, Ord. The Alamo isn't worth the bones of a Britainer, either."
343: 
344: "Colonel Bowie, please," Ord cried. "You don't understand! You _must_
345: defend the Alamo! This is the turning point in the winning of the west! If
346: Houston is beaten, Texas will never join the Union! There will be no
347: Mexican War. No California, no nation stretching from sea to shining sea!
348: This is the Americans' manifest destiny. You are the hope of the future ...
349: you will save the world from Hitler, from Bolshevism--"
350: 
351: "Crazy as a hoot owl," Bowie said sadly. "Ord, you and Travis got to look
352: at it both ways. We ain't all in the right in this war--we Americans got
353: our faults, too."
354: 
355: "But you are free men," Ord whispered. "Vulgar, opinionated, brutal--but
356: free! You are still better than any breed who kneels to tyranny--"
357: 
358: Crockett came in. "O.K., Jim."
359: 
360: "How'd it go?"
361: 
362: "Fifty-one per cent for hightailin' it right now."
363: 
364: Bowie smiled. "That's a flat majority. Let's make tracks."
365: 
366: "Comin', Bill?" Crockett asked. "You're O.K., but you just don't know how
367: to be one of the boys. You got to learn that no dog is better'n any other."
368: 
369: "No," Travis croaked hoarsely. "I stay. Stay or go, we shall all die like
370: dogs, anyway. Boys, for the last time! Don't reveal our weakness to the
371: enemy--"
372: 
373: "What weakness? We're stronger than them. Americans could whip the Mexicans
374: any day, if we wanted to. But the thing to do is make 'em talk, not fight.
375: So long, Bill."
376: 
377: The two big men stepped outside. In the night there was a sudden clatter of
378: hoofs as the Texans mounted and rode. From across the river came a brief
379: spatter of musket fire, then silence. In the dark, there had been no
380: difficulty in breaking through the Mexican lines.
381: 
382: Inside the chapel, John Ord's mouth hung slackly. He muttered, "Am I
383: insane? It didn't happen this way--it couldn't! The books can't be _that_
384: wrong--"
385: 
386: In the candlelight, Travis hung his head. "We tried, John. Perhaps it was a
387: forlorn hope at best. Even if we had defeated Santa Anna, or delayed him, I
388: do not think the Indian Nations would have let Houston get help from the
389: United States."
390: 
391: Ord continued his dazed muttering, hardly hearing.
392: 
393: "We need a contiguous frontier with Texas," Travis continued slowly, just
394: above a whisper. "But we Americans have never broken a treaty with the
395: Indians, and pray God we never shall. _We_ aren't like the Mexicans, always
396: pushing, always grabbing off New Mexico, Arizona, California. _We_ aren't
397: colonial oppressors, thank God! No, it wouldn't have worked out, even if we
398: American immigrants had secured our rights in Texas--" He lifted a short,
399: heavy, percussion pistol in his hand and cocked it. "I hate to say it, but
400: perhaps if we hadn't taken Payne and Jefferson so seriously--if we could
401: only have paid lip service, and done what we really wanted to do, in our
402: hearts ... no matter. I won't live to see our final disgrace."
403: 
404: He put the pistol to his head and blew out his brains.
405: 
406:        *       *       *       *       *
407: 
408: Ord was still gibbering when the Mexican cavalry stormed into the old
409: mission, pulling down the flag and seizing him, dragging him before the
410: resplendent little general in green and gold.
411: 
412: Since he was the only prisoner, Santa Anna questioned Ord carefully. When
413: the sharp point of a bayonet had been thrust half an inch into his stomach,
414: the Britainer seemed to come around. When he started speaking, and the
415: Mexicans realized he was English, it went better with him. Ord was
416: obviously mad, it seemed to Santa Anna, but since he spoke English and
417: seemed educated, he could be useful. Santa Anna didn't mind the raving; he
418: understood all about Napoleon's detention camps and what they had done to
419: Britainers over there. In fact, Santa Anna was thinking of setting up a
420: couple of those camps himself. When they had milked Ord dry, they threw him
421: on a horse and took him along.
422: 
423: Thus John Ord had an excellent view of the battlefield when Santa Anna's
424: cannon broke the American lines south of the Trinity. Unable to get his men
425: across to safety, Sam Houston died leading the last, desperate charge
426: against the Mexican regulars. After that, the American survivors were too
427: tired to run from the cavalry that pinned them against the flooding river.
428: Most of them died there. Santa Anna expressed complete indifference to what
429: happened to the Texans' women and children.
430: 
431: Mexican soldiers found Jim Bowie hiding in a hut, wearing a plain linen
432: tunic and pretending to be a civilian. They would not have discovered his
433: identity had not some of the Texan women cried out, "Colonel Bowie--Colonel
434: Bowie!" as he was led into the Mexican camp.
435: 
436: He was hauled before Santa Anna, and Ord was summoned to watch. "Well, don
437: Jaime," Santa Anna remarked, "You have been a foolish man. I promised your
438: wife's uncle to send you to Acapulco safely, though of course your lands
439: are forfeit. You understand we must have lands for the veterans' program
440: when this campaign is over--" Santa Anna smiled then. "Besides, since Ord
441: here has told me how instrumental you were in the abandonment of the Alamo,
442: I think the Emperor will agree to mercy in your case. You know, don Jaime,
443: your compatriots had me worried back there. The Alamo might have been a
444: tough nut to crack ... _pues_, no matter."
445: 
446: And since Santa Anna had always been broadminded, not objecting to light
447: skin or immigrant background, he invited Bowie to dinner that night.
448: 
449:        *       *       *       *       *
450: 
451: Santa Anna turned to Ord. "But if we could catch this rascally war
452: criminal, Crockett ... however, I fear he has escaped us. He slipped over
453: the river with a fake passport, and the Indians have interned him."
454: 
455: "Sí, _Señor Presidente_," Ord said dully.
456: 
457: "Please, don't call me that," Santa Anna cried, looking around. "True, many
458: of us officers have political ambitions, but Emperor Iturbide is old and
459: vain. It could mean my head--"
460: 
461: Suddenly, Ord's head was erect, and the old, clear light was in his blue
462: eyes. "Now I understand!" he shouted. "I thought Travis was raving back
463: there, before he shot himself--and your talk of the Emperor! American
464: respect for Indian rights! Jeffersonian form of government! Oh, those
465: ponces who peddled me that X-4-A--the _track jumper_! I'm not back in my
466: own past. I've jumped the time track--_I'm back in a screaming
467: alternate!_"
468: 
469: "Please, not so loud, _Señor_ Ord," Santa Anna sighed. "Now, we must shoot
470: a few more American officers, of course. I regret this, you understand, and
471: I shall no doubt be much criticized in French Canada and Russia, where
472: there are still civilized values. But we must establish the Republic of the
473: Empire once and for all upon this continent, that aristocratic tyranny
474: shall not perish from the earth. Of course, as an Englishman, you
475: understand perfectly, Señor Ord."
476: 
477: "Of course, excellency," Ord said.
478: 
479: "There are soft hearts--soft heads, I say--in Mexico who cry for civil
480: rights for the Americans. But I must make sure that Mexican dominance is
481: never again threatened north of the Rio Grande."
482: 
483: "_Seguro_, excellency," Ord said, suddenly. If the bloody X-4-A _had_
484: jumped the track, there was no getting back, none at all. He was stuck
485: here. Ord's blue eyes narrowed. "After all, it ... it is manifest destiny
486: that the Latin peoples of North America meet at the center of the
487: continent. Canada and Mexico shall share the Mississippi."
488: 
489: Santa Anna's dark eyes glowed. "You say what I have often thought. You are
490: a man of vision, and much sense. You realize the _Indios_ must go, whether
491: they were here first or not. I think I will make you my secretary, with the
492: rank of captain."
493: 
494: "_Gracias_, Excellency."
495: 
496: "Now, let us write my communique to the capital, _Capitán_ Ord. We must
497: describe how the American abandonment of the Alamo allowed me to press the
498: traitor Houston so closely he had no chance to maneuver his men into the
499: trap he sought. _Ay, Capitán_, it is a cardinal principle of the
500: Anglo-Saxons, to get themselves into a trap from which they must fight
501: their way out. This I never let them do, which is why I succeed where
502: others fail ... you said something, _Capitán_?"
503: 
504: "_Sí_, Excellency. I said, I shall title our communique: 'Remember the
505: Alamo,'" Ord said, standing at attention.
506: 
507: "_Bueno!_ You have a gift for words. Indeed, if ever we feel the _gringos_
508: are too much for us, your words shall once again remind us of the truth!"
509: Santa Anna smiled. "I think I shall make you a major. You have indeed
510: coined a phrase which shall live in history forever!"
511:

50: A good many of my radical friends express a certain kindly
51: condescension when they speak of Edward Bellamy's "Looking Backward."
52: 
53: "Of course you know," they say, "that it really isn't first-rate
54: economics."
55: 
56: And yet in further conversation I have known a very large number of
57: these same somewhat scornful Socialists to admit, "You know, the first
58: thing that got me started to thinking about Socialism was Bellamy's
59: 'Looking Backward.'"
60: 
61: From the beginning it has been a highly provocative book. It is now.
62: Many of the questions both of mood and technique are even more
63: pertinent in the year 1931 than they were in 1887. A critic of the
64: _Boston Transcript_ said, when the novel first appeared, that the new
65: State imagined by Bellamy was all very well, but that the author lost
66: much of his effectiveness by putting his Utopia a scant fifty years
67: ahead, and that he might much better have made it seventy-five
68: centuries.
69: 
70: It is true that the fifty years assigned for changing the world
71: utterly are almost gone by now. Not everything which was predicted in
72: "Looking Backward" has come to pass. But the laugh is not against
73: Bellamy, but against his critic. Some of the things which must have
74: seemed most improbable of all to the _Transcript_ man of 1887 are now
75: actually in being.

20: Nobody in the neighborhood was surprised when Martin's mother
21: disappeared and Ninian came to take care of him. Mothers had a way
22: of disappearing around those parts and the kids were often better
23: off without them. Martin was no exception. He'd never had it this
24: good while he was living with his old lady. As for his father, Martin
25: had never had one. He'd been a war baby, born of one of the tides of
26: soldiers--enemies and allies, both--that had engulfed the country in
27: successive waves and bought or taken the women. So there was no trouble
28: that way.
29: 
30: Sometimes he wondered who Ninian really was. Obviously that story
31: about her coming from the future was just a gag. Besides, if she really
32: was his great-great-grand-daughter, as she said, why would she tell
33: him to call her "_Aunt Ninian_"? Maybe he was only eleven, but he'd
34: been around and he knew just what the score was. At first he'd thought
35: maybe she was some new kind of social worker, but she acted a little
36: too crazy for that.
37: 
38: He loved to bait her, as he had loved to bait his mother. It was safer
39: with Ninian, though, because when he pushed her too far, she would cry
40: instead of mopping up the floor with him.
41: 
42: "But I can't understand," he would say, keeping his face straight. "Why
43: do you have to come from the future to protect me against your cousin
44: Conrad?"
45: 
46: "Because he's coming to kill you."
47: 
48: "Why should he kill me? I ain't done him nothing."
49: 
50: Ninian sighed. "He's dissatisfied with the current social order and
51: killing you is part of an elaborate plan he's formulated to change it.
52: You wouldn't understand."
53: 
54: "You're damn right. I _don't_ understand. What's it all about in
55: straight gas?"
56: 
57: "Oh, just don't ask any questions," Ninian said petulantly. "When you
58: get older, someone will explain the whole thing to you."

62: So Martin held his peace, because, on the whole, he liked things the
63: way they were. Ninian really was the limit, though. All the people he
64: knew lived in scabrous tenement apartments like his, but she seemed to
65: think it was disgusting.
66: 
67: "So if you don't like it, clean it up," he suggested.
68: 
69: She looked at him as if he were out of his mind.
70: 
71: "Hire a maid, then!" he jeered.
72: 
73: And darned if that dope didn't go out and get a woman to come clean up
74: the place! He was so embarrassed, he didn't even dare show his face in
75: the streets--especially with the women buttonholing him and demanding
76: to know what gave. They tried talking to Ninian, but she certainly knew
77: how to give them the cold shoulder.
78: 
79: One day the truant officer came to ask why Martin hadn't been coming
80: to school. Very few of the neighborhood kids attended classes very
81: regularly, so this was just routine. But Ninian didn't know that and
82: she went into a real tizzy, babbling that Martin had been sick and
83: would make up the work. Martin nearly did get sick from laughing so
84: hard inside.

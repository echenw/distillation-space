1041|The first matter to which I gave consideration, after we were fairly
1042|under way, and had parted company with the longboat, was that of food
1043|and drink; and I began by taking stock roughly of what we had, and
1044|jotting down the items in my pocket-book.  To begin with, we had four
1045|five-gallon breakers of fresh water--twenty gallons in all.  Then we had
1046|two sacks of cabin bread, which, by a partial count, I estimated to
1047|contain about three hundred biscuits altogether.  And in addition to
1048|these we had one dozen tins of ox tongue; six small tins of potted
1049|meats; four jars of marmalade and two of jam; two bottles of pickles;
1050|four bottles of lime juice; one bottle of brandy; and two bottles of
1051|rum.  When I had jotted everything down I made a few calculations, and
1052|then I spoke.
1053|
1054|"Shipmates," I said,--"and I include you, Mr Cunningham, in the term,
1055|for this misfortune puts us all upon the same footing--you no doubt
1056|heard Mr Bligh say, a little while ago, that according to his reckoning
1057|we are somewhere about twelve hundred miles from Rio, which is our
1058|nearest port.  That means a twelve days' voyage, with a fair wind all
1059|the time, blowing fresh enough to keep us going, hour after hour, at the
1060|rate of five knots.  Now, those of us who have used the sea don't need
1061|to be told that such a favourable condition of affairs is so exceedingly
1062|unlikely that it is scarcely worth talking about.  To begin with, we are
1063|making a bad start, for instead of doing our five knots we are doing
1064|little if anything more than half that, with every prospect of a flat
1065|calm within the next three or four hours.  Therefore I think it will be
1066|wise of us to recognise, at the outset, that our voyage is a good deal
1067|more likely to take twenty days than it is to be accomplished in ten.
1068|
1069|"Of course, in saying this I am regarding the matter from its most
1070|unfavourable point of view.  I remember that we have had easterly winds
1071|without a break ever since we crossed the line, and it may be that the
1072|Trades are extending unusually far south just now, and that we are still
1073|on the southerly fringe of them.  If this should prove to be the case we
1074|shall be all right, for by steering a west and by no'th course we shall
1075|be edging to the nor'ard and working our way back into the permanent
1076|trade winds.  But, on the other hand, this easterly wind may not be the
1077|trade wind at all--and my own opinion is that it is not--in which case
1078|we may expect a westerly breeze--that is to say, a foul wind--at any
1079|moment; and I think we should only be acting with common prudence to
1080|take such a probability into consideration.
1081|
1082|"Now, this brings me to the question of food and water.  As you have
1083|seen, I have been taking stock of what we have, and making a few
1084|calculations, with the following result.  First, with regard to the
1085|fresh water.  We have just twenty gallons of it, or one hundred and
1086|sixty pints.  If we could be certain of making our voyage in ten days
1087|that amount of water would afford sixteen pints per day to be equally
1088|divided between the five of us, which is a fraction over three pints per
1089|day per man, or, say, half a pint at each of three meals and another
1090|half-pint at three intervals between meals.  Little enough, you will
1091|say.  Very true; yet I think we must endeavour to do with less.  We must
1092|try to be satisfied with four half-pints per day of twenty-four hours
1093|per man, by which means we shall be able to make our water last sixteen
1094|days, and in sixteen days many things may happen: we may end our voyage,
1095|if we have luck; or we may be picked up; or we may have rain enough to
1096|enable us to replenish our water supply.  But since neither of these
1097|things may happen, we ought, in common prudence, to determine at the
1098|outset not to drink more than four half-pints per man per day; and I
1099|think we may be able to manage upon that without any very great
1100|hardship.  What say you?"
1101|
1102|"I think we can manage it, if we set our minds to do it," at once
1103|answered Mr Cunningham, and after a little further talk the boatswain,
1104|carpenter, and sailmaker also agreed to make the attempt.  In the same
1105|way we arrived at a determination to be satisfied with four biscuits per
1106|day each, with a suitable proportion of tongue, potted meat, jam, and
1107|what not; and we also agreed upon the quantity of spirits which was to
1108|constitute each man's daily allowance, Cunningham being of opinion that
1109|a very small allowance of stimulant would be almost a necessity, seeing
1110|that our food was to be so restricted in quantity.  And then, having
1111|settled this important question, we piped to supper, each man receiving
1112|the exact quantity of food agreed upon; and when we had finished we were
1113|all of the one opinion, namely, that although our appetites were far
1114|from being satisfied, it would be quite possible for us to sustain life
1115|under such conditions for a fortnight or three weeks without serious
1116|deterioration of either health or strength.
